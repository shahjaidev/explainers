Fireworks AI blog · March 2026

Frontier RL is cheaper than you think

The story everyone tells is that training a frontier model with reinforcement learning needs one giant, co-located GPU cluster. Fireworks argues the whole story rests on a single assumption: that you must ship a full terabyte of weights to every rollout machine every time the model updates. You don't. About 98% of the weights don't change from one step to the next, so you ship the 2% that did, hot-load it into servers that keep running, and tolerate rollouts that are a few minutes behind. Once that's true, the rollout half of RL can live on whatever GPUs are available, wherever they are.

01 · The claim in one breath

RL has two halves, and only one of them needs the big cluster

An RL training step for a language model is a loop. The model generates lots of answers (rollouts), those answers get scored, and a trainer nudges the weights toward the answers that scored well. Then the rollout machines need the new weights, and the loop repeats.

The trainer genuinely needs tightly connected GPUs: it moves gradients and optimizer state around constantly. But the rollout half is just inference. It is also where most of the wall-clock time and most of the GPU-hours go, because generating thousands of long answers takes far longer than one weight update. Fireworks' point is that the expensive half is the easy half to spread out, as long as you can get fresh weights to it cheaply.

Fireworks looked at a 50-step window of a real run on a roughly 1 TB model. Between any two consecutive checkpoints, more than 98% of the bf16 weights were bit-for-bit identical. Not "close". Identical. So the difference between checkpoint t and checkpoint t+1 can be written as a short list of "these positions changed, to these values", and that list averaged 20.3 GiB. Over the whole window that cut cross-region transfer volume by about 94% versus copying the full model each time.

Why do so few weights change? The short version: RL learning rates are tiny, and bf16 only has 7 bits of mantissa. Most individual updates are smaller than the gap between two representable bf16 numbers, so after rounding, the stored weight is exactly what it was before. The math tab works through this.

This is lossless. The rollout servers reconstruct the exact same weights the trainer has. Nothing is quantized or approximated to get the 50× saving. The saving comes from precision the trainer already threw away when it cast to bf16.

03 · The three mechanisms

What has to be true for distributed rollouts to work

MECHANISM 1

Compact checkpoint deltas

Encode only the weights that changed since the last checkpoint the fleet already has. Fireworks calls its format ARC2 (arc_v2) and reports 20× or better compression on typical RL steps. Deltas ride over ordinary object storage, so no special networking between trainer and inference.

MECHANISM 2

Hot-load updates

Rollout servers apply the delta to weights already resident in GPU memory instead of restarting and re-reading a terabyte from disk. The fleet keeps serving; the gap between "trainer finished a step" and "rollouts use the new policy" shrinks from a restart cycle to a load of a few GB.

MECHANISM 3

Bounded staleness

Rollouts in flight were generated by a policy a step or two old. That is fine if you cap how old, because standard off-policy corrections (the importance-sampling ratio in PPO/GRPO-style losses) already account for it. Async RL research shows matched final performance with modest staleness. The post describes the result as minutes-level staleness across clusters.

04 · Why this changes the economics

Mega clusters are scarce. Medium clusters are everywhere.

The market rarely offers a single contiguous block of thousands of the newest GPUs on short notice, and when it does, you pay a premium and wait. Smaller pools in many regions are plentiful and cheaper. If the rollout fleet can be stitched together from three or four of those, you stop waiting for the mega cluster and start training.

There is a second effect: rollout inference and production inference are the same workload. Fireworks reports that Cursor trained Composer 2 with rollouts spread across 3 to 4 clusters worldwide, with training traffic and production traffic sharing one GPU pool, fed by delta-compressed weight updates. Capacity that would otherwise sit idle at off-peak hours becomes rollout capacity.

Latency is not the obstacle it sounds like. The extra 43 to 145 ms between regions is paid once per request, not once per token. A rollout that takes a minute to generate barely notices a tenth of a second of round-trip. What actually matters is weight-update bandwidth, and deltas fix that.

06 · If you remember five things

The takeaways

  1. The mega-cluster story has one load-bearing assumption: that every weight update means moving a full terabyte to every rollout machine. Remove it and the story collapses.
  2. RL updates are sparse at the bit level. More than 98% of bf16 weights are unchanged between consecutive checkpoints, so a lossless delta is about 2% of the model (20.3 GiB vs 1024 GiB in the sample run).
  3. Hot-loading keeps the fleet warm. Apply the delta in place instead of restarting servers, so the rollout policy lags the trainer by minutes, not by a redeploy.
  4. A few minutes of staleness is a known, solved problem. Off-policy corrections already in the loss absorb it; async RL work shows no loss in final performance at bounded lag.
  5. Rollouts dominate cost, and rollouts are just inference. Spread them across regions and share them with production traffic. Keep only the trainer on tightly coupled hardware.
Math 1 · Why 98% of weights don't move

bf16 can't see small changes

A bf16 number has 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, and 7 mantissa bits. Seven mantissa bits means there are only 128 evenly spaced values between any power of two and the next one. So the gap between neighbouring bf16 numbers near a weight w is roughly |w| / 128, and to tip over to the next representable value an update has to move at least half that gap.

bf16 spacing near w ≈ |w| / 27 = |w| / 128
update is absorbed (weight stays bit-identical) if |Δw| < |w| / 256
update is visible otherwise

Now compare that to how big an RL update actually is. With Adam, each step moves a weight by roughly the learning rate (the gradient is normalised away). RL post-training learning rates are tiny, on the order of 10−6. A typical weight is around 10−2. Plug in:

threshold = |w| / 256 ≈ 0.01 / 256 ≈ 4 × 10−5
Adam step ≈ lr ≈ 3 × 10−6
3 × 10−6 ≪ 4 × 10−5 → the bf16 weight does not change
Plain English: the trainer is nudging each weight by about one-thirteenth of the smallest nudge bf16 can record. Most nudges round away. The trainer keeps the exact fp32 copy, so the nudges accumulate and eventually one of them tips a weight over the edge. That trickle is the ~2% that changes each step.

Math 2 · How big is a delta

Delta size = changed fraction × model size, plus addresses

full checkpoint = 1024 GiB
changed fraction = 1.98%
changed values = 0.0198 × 1024 GiB ≈ 20.3 GiB ← the post's reported average

over 50 steps:
  full copies = 50 × 1024 = 51,200 GiB
  deltas ≈ 50 × 20.3 ≈ 1,015 GiB (≈ 98% less)
  reported ≈ 94% less, once the seed copy and fatter-than-average steps are counted

One subtlety: a delta has to say where each changed value goes, not just what it is. Naively that doubles the size (a 4-byte index per 2-byte value). The trick is to store the gaps between changed positions instead of absolute positions, and encode those gaps with a variable-length code. Since about one in fifty weights changes, gaps are small numbers that fit in a byte, and the addressing overhead becomes a minor fraction of the payload. That is what "delta-encoded variable-length indexing" means in the post.

Plain English: instead of writing "position 1,000,000 changed, position 1,000,047 changed", you write "changed; skip 47; changed". Small skips take fewer bits than big addresses.
Math 3 · Transfer time

Seconds, not minutes, per region

transfer time = bytes × 8 / link speed
full: 1024 GiB × 8 / 10 Gb/s ≈ 880 s ≈ 14.7 min
delta: 20.3 GiB × 8 / 10 Gb/s ≈ 17 s

With a full copy, each weight update costs a quarter of an hour per region on a 10 Gb/s inter-region link. Against a training step that itself takes several minutes, the fleet would be permanently behind. With a delta, the update is a coffee-break pause even on a modest link, and nearly instant on 100 Gb/s.

Math 4 · Why staleness is survivable

The loss already corrects for an old policy

If a rollout was sampled from the policy as it was k steps ago (call it πold), and you are now updating the current policy πθ, you are learning from slightly off-policy data. PPO- and GRPO-style objectives handle exactly this. Each token's contribution is reweighted by how much more or less likely the current policy would be to produce it, and that weight is clipped so a single stale sample cannot swing the update.

ratio r = πθ(token | context) / πold(token | context)
objective L = mean[ min( r · A , clip(r, 1−ε, 1+ε) · A ) ]
A = advantage (how much better this answer was than the group average); ε ≈ 0.2

When the rollout policy is close to the current one, r is near 1 and the correction is nearly a no-op. The further behind the rollouts fall, the more the ratios spread and the more of the batch the clip quietly discards. So staleness has a cost, and it grows with k. The engineering job is to bound k at a small number, which is what fast deltas plus hot-loading buy you. Studies of fully asynchronous RL find that with existing off-policy corrections and a staleness cap of a few steps, final performance matches synchronous training.

Plain English: the model is allowed to learn from answers written by a slightly older version of itself, as long as "slightly" stays slight. Deltas and hot-loading are what keep it slight.
Math 5 · Latency vs. throughput

145 ms is nothing against a one-minute rollout

overhead = round-trip latency / rollout duration
Frankfurt: 0.145 s / 60 s ≈ 0.24%
Ohio: 0.043 s / 60 s ≈ 0.07%

The orchestrator sends a prompt to a region and gets a finished rollout back. Latency is paid at the start and end of that round trip, while the GPU time in the middle is thousands of decoded tokens. Agentic rollouts with tool calls add more round trips, but each tool call is typically served near the rollout GPUs, not back across the ocean. The quantity that scales with the number of GPUs is weight-update bandwidth, and that is the one deltas attack.

Math 6 · The cost picture

Where the GPU-hours go

cost per step ≈ rollout GPU-hours × rollout $/hr + trainer GPU-hours × trainer $/hr
rollout GPU-hours ≫ trainer GPU-hours (generation is long; the gradient step is short)

Because the first term dominates, the lever with the most weight is the price and availability of rollout GPUs. Those can be any inference-grade GPUs, in any region, bought at whatever rate is available, and shared with production traffic. The trainer term stays on premium interconnected hardware, but it is the small term. That asymmetry is the whole "cheaper than you think".

Glossary

Terms used in the post

Rollout
One generated answer (or multi-turn trajectory) sampled from the current policy, later scored to produce a reward.
Checkpoint
A full snapshot of the model's weights at a given training step. In the sample run, 1024 GiB.
Checkpoint delta
The lossless difference between two checkpoints: the list of changed positions and their new values. About 2% of a full checkpoint in RL post-training.
Hot-load
Applying new weights to a running inference server in place, without restarting it or re-reading the full model from disk.
Staleness
How many trainer steps behind the current policy the weights used for a rollout are. "Bounded" means capped at a fixed small number.
Asynchronous RL
Running rollout generation and weight updates at the same time instead of one after the other, accepting bounded staleness in exchange for keeping both kinds of GPU busy.
bf16
A 16-bit floating-point format with 8 exponent bits and 7 mantissa bits. Coarse enough that most tiny RL updates round away to nothing.
Off-policy correction
Reweighting each sample by the ratio of new-policy to old-policy probability (clipped), so learning from a slightly older policy's rollouts stays unbiased enough.
ARC2 / arc_v2
Fireworks' name for its lossless compressed incremental snapshot format, used to ship deltas over ordinary object storage.
