Four over n.

4n= 1x+ 1y+ 1z

The Erdős–Straus conjecture says this equation has a solution in positive integers for every n ≥ 2. It has been open since 1948. This page is a working session against it: everything provable gets proved, the frontier gets recomputed from scratch — every prime to 10⁹ certified in 12.8 seconds on this machine — and the exact shape of the remaining wall is laid bare. Spoiler: the conjecture survives the attack. The interesting part is why.

01 The problem

A 3,600-year-old data format meets a 1948 question.

Ancient Egyptian scribes wrote fractions as sums of distinct unit fractions — reciprocals of integers. The Rhind papyrus (~1650 BCE) opens with a table doing exactly this for 2/n. Every positive rational can be written this way (Fibonacci's greedy algorithm already proves it), so the modern questions are about how few terms you need.

For fractions 3/n, three terms always suffice, easily. In 1948 Paul Erdős and Ernst Straus asked the next question up and got stuck — and everyone since has stayed stuck:

Conjecture (Erdős–Straus). For every integer n ≥ 2 there exist positive integers x, y, z with 4/n = 1/x + 1/y + 1/z.

Concretely: 4/5 = 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/20, and 4/7 = 1/2 + 1/15 + 1/210. Small cases are trivial to find. The conjecture is that the supply never runs out — that no n, however contrived, needs a fourth term. (Whether the three denominators must be distinct turns out not to matter: any repeated term splits via 1/q = 1/(q+1) + 1/(q(q+1)).)

Why is this hard? Because 4/n sits at the exact boundary where greedy algorithms stop working and the problem becomes genuinely arithmetic — solvability starts depending on the multiplicative structure of n, in particular on which quadratic residues n occupies. That connection is the spine of this page.

02 First reduction

Only primes matter.

If the equation is solvable for n, it is solvable for every multiple of n — just scale all three denominators:

proved on the spot

If 4/n = 1/x + 1/y + 1/z, then for any positive integer t, 4/(tn) = 1/(tx) + 1/(ty) + 1/(tz). So a counterexample, if one exists, has the property that all of its divisors > 1 are counterexamples too — meaning the smallest counterexample is prime. To prove the conjecture, it suffices to prove it for primes.

From here on, n = p is prime. And immediately, most primes fall to one-line identities.

03 The identity machine

Five sieves, and the six classes that slip through all of them.

An identity is a formula that solves the equation for an entire residue class at once. Three of them are short enough to prove right here. Write the target prime as shown; each line is checked by putting everything over a common denominator.

Identity 1 · p ≡ 2 (mod 3), so p = 3k + 2
4p= 1p+ 1k+1+ 1p(k+1)

Check: the last two terms sum to (p+1)/(p(k+1)) = 3(k+1)/(p(k+1)) = 3/p, since p+1 = 3(k+1). Add 1/p to get 4/p. ∎

Identity 2 · p ≡ 3 (mod 4), so p = 4k + 3
4p= 1k+1+ 1p(k+1)

Check: the sum is (p+1)/(p(k+1)) = 4(k+1)/(p(k+1)) = 4/p, since p+1 = 4(k+1). Two terms — split either one to make three. ∎

Identity 3 · p ≡ 5 (mod 8), so p = 8k + 5
4p= 12k+2+ 1p(k+1)+ 12p(k+1)

Check: 4/p − 1/(2k+2) = (8k+8−p)/(p(2k+2)) = 3/(2p(k+1)), and the last two terms sum to exactly 3/(2p(k+1)). ∎ The trick: because p ≡ 5 (mod 8), the leftover numerator 3 lands on an even denominator, and 3/(2t) = 1/t + 1/(2t) always.

These three identities alone kill every prime except p ≡ 1 (mod 24). Mordell, following the same playbook with heavier machinery, added families for two classes mod 5 and three classes mod 7. Compose everything and the natural home of the problem becomes residues mod 840 = 8 · 3 · 5 · 7. Step through the sieve:

Run all five stages. The survivors are 1, 121, 169, 289, 361, 529 — that is, 1², 11², 13², 17², 19², 23². The classes that defeat every known identity are exactly the perfect squares coprime to 840. That is not a coincidence, and it is the reason the problem is still open. Section 04 explains.

So the state of the art since the 1960s: the conjecture is a theorem for every n outside six thin residue classes mod 840 — about 3.1% of primes. Everything since has been either computation (pushing the verified bound) or analysis (proving failures, if any, are extraordinarily rare).

04 The wall

Why identities can never finish the job.

It's tempting to think we just haven't found the right sixth identity. That hope is dead — provably. To see why, look at what an identity actually is under the hood.

Take a solution where two denominators are multiples of p (a Type I solution — nearly all solutions for primes are of this shape). Write it as 4/p = 1/x + 1/(py) + 1/(pz). Subtract 1/x, and with a = 4x − p the equation collapses to 1/y + 1/z = a/x. A short divisor argument turns that into an exact criterion:

the engine · proved in the appendix of every verification paper

For prime p and x > p/4, put a = 4x − p. A Type I solution with first denominator x exists iff x² has a divisor m ≡ −x (mod a). Then y = (x+m)/a and z = x(x+m)/(am).

Every classical identity is this criterion with the divisor m chosen once, symbolically, for a whole class. And there sits the obstruction, in that minus sign: the criterion demands that some divisor of a square be congruent to −x. Divisors of squares are heavily biased toward quadratic residues; the minus sign asks for the opposite side of the ledger. When p — and hence x, a polynomial in p — ranges over a residue class that is itself a perfect square, the two sides can never be matched uniformly. Schinzel made this precise:

Theorem (Schinzel). There is no identity — no triple of integer-valued polynomials x(n), y(n), z(n) solving the equation on an entire class n ≡ r (mod m) — when r is a quadratic residue mod m. The six surviving classes mod 840 are squares, so no finite set of identities, mod 840 or mod anything else, can ever cover them.

This is the honest shape of the problem. Not "we haven't been clever enough with formulas" but "formulas are provably the wrong weapon." Each individual prime in the six classes falls easily — as the computation below shows, almost embarrassingly easily — but they must be felled one at a time, each with its own bespoke divisor. A proof needs a fundamentally non-uniform idea, and nobody has one.

05 This session's computation

Every prime to 10⁹, certified from scratch in 12.8 seconds.

Rather than take the literature's word for it, this page re-derives the frontier. A small C program (written for this page) sieves all 50,847,534 primes below 10⁹ and, for each of the 12,710,866 primes ≡ 1 (mod 12) — every prime not already settled by Identities 1–3 above — runs the divisor criterion directly: walk x = ⌈p/4⌉, ⌈p/4⌉+1, …, and at each step search the divisors of x² for one that is ≡ −x (mod 4x−p). Every hit is re-verified in exact 128-bit arithmetic before being counted, and the record-setting certificates were re-verified a second time in exact rational arithmetic in a separate language. Result: a certificate for every single prime. Zero failures.

Two structural facts jump out of the run.

1 · The problem is "almost too easy" pointwise

The very first candidate x works for 79% of the searched primes; the deepest search in the entire run — prime 168,478,249 — needed just 33 candidates. The average was 0.28 extra steps.

How quickly certificates are found

Share of the 12,710,866 searched primes (≡ 1 mod 12, p < 10⁹) certified within the first k candidate values of x.

2 · Difficulty decays geometrically, with arithmetic fine structure

Each certificate has a level a = 4x − p — the size of the numerator the divisor criterion had to clear. Level 3 catches ~79%, level 7 catches most of the rest, and each further level shrinks by roughly 5–7×. But the decay isn't smooth: level 27 = 3³ catches fewer primes than level 31, and 35 = 5·7 fewer than 39 — when the level's own factorization is "used up," the congruence it imposes gets harder to satisfy. The wall has texture.

One more consistency check the run provides: the six hard classes mod 840 should each contain the same density of primes (Dirichlet), and indeed the counts land within 0.2% of each other — 264,770 · 264,645 · 264,608 · 264,716 · 264,503 · 264,339, totalling 1,587,581 hard-class primes, 3.12% of all primes below 10⁹ (the theoretical density is 6/192 = 3.125%).

07 The frontier

Where the problem actually stands.

Three genuinely different kinds of progress exist, and it's worth keeping them separate:

Computation. Swett verified to 10¹⁴ (1999); Salez to 10¹⁷ (2014). Any counterexample is a prime ≡ 1², 11², 13², 17², 19², or 23² (mod 840) larger than that. The pointwise statistics above explain why hardly anyone expects one to exist.

Density. Vaughan (1970) proved the number of potential counterexamples below N is at most N·exp(−c(log N)^(2/3)) — not just measure zero among integers, but sparser than any power saving. The conjecture is a theorem for "almost all" n in a strong quantitative sense.

Structure. Elsholtz and Tao (2013) counted solutions rather than solvability: the number of representations of 4/p fluctuates but is polylogarithmic in p on average — for a typical prime there are not one but hundreds of certificates. The conjecture asks that a quantity which is almost always large is never zero — exactly the kind of statement (like Goldbach's conjecture) that analytic methods approach but cannot close.

The same story repeats for the natural generalization k/n = 1/x + 1/y + 1/z: Sierpiński conjectured it for k = 5 (1956), Schinzel for every fixed k with n large — all open, all blocked by the same square-classes wall.

not done — by anyone, ever

  • A proof for even one of the six square classes mod 840.
  • Any workaround for Schinzel's obstruction — the reason no list of formulas can finish.
  • Any bound forcing solvability pointwise (density results allow exceptions; computation only pushes the floor).
  • The conjecture itself. It is exactly as open as it was in 1948 — just better mapped.
