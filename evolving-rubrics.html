Rubrics that move

DR Tulu made the grading criteria a living object that co-evolves with the policy. Kimi K3 made writing them a mandatory step of the judge's job. Two answers to the same problem: on non-verifiable tasks, a fixed rubric goes stale the moment the model gets good at it.

The problem

You cannot check a research report against an answer key

RL with verifiable rewards works because a string match or a unit test is a free, incorruptible grader. Most of what we now want models to do — write a report, produce a deliverable, synthesize a literature — has no such grader.

The obvious substitute is a rubric: a written list of criteria an LLM judge scores against. DR Tulu's authors argue that the obvious version of this fails twice over. Desiderata for long-form answers are under-specified, so no fixed list captures them. And a rubric written closed-book, from a judge's parametric knowledge alone, "risks missing evidence, subtle errors, and [is] vulnerable to reward hacking."

There is a third failure that only shows up during training. A criterion every rollout satisfies contributes nothing to the gradient. As the policy improves, more and more of a fixed rubric becomes exactly that — a list of things the model already does. The reward signal quietly flattens.

The fix is to give the rubric writer information the policy does not have, and to keep rewriting.

RLER — Reinforcement Learning with Evolving Rubrics — supplies two kinds of privileged information. Retrieved external evidence, so criteria can be grounded in facts the policy may have missed; and multiple sampled rollouts seen side by side, so criteria can be written to discriminate between them. That asymmetry is the generation–verification gap the whole method runs on.

Initialization

Illustrative reconstruction of the mechanism described in §3.1. Before training, a search over the prompt seeds persistent, evidence-grounded criteria. At every GRPO step the rubric LM reads the prompt, all rollouts and the current pool, then writes positive criteria for knowledge the policy just discovered and negative ones for hacks it just attempted. Criteria with zero score variance across rollouts are struck out and dropped; the rest are ranked by standard deviation and truncated to the top K.

The two rubric types do different work. Positive rubrics chase the frontier — they encode things the policy has started finding but has not yet made habitual. Negative rubrics chase the exploits: the paper's own example is a model copying retrieved text verbatim to drive citation precision to 1.0, which becomes a criterion you lose points for.

Variance-based pruning is what keeps the buffer honest. A rubric all eight rollouts satisfy has standard deviation zero and is deleted — not because it stopped being true, but because it stopped being informative. The buffer converges on whatever the model is currently bad at.

Does it hold up

Grounding matters more than evolution, but both are real

The cleanest ablation isolates the rubric source. Starting from the same intermediate SFT checkpoint, the team ran 500 RL steps against three kinds of static rubric and measured the four-benchmark average.

Table 3. Four-benchmark average after 500 RL steps from the SFT v0.1 checkpoint. Search-grounded rubrics beat both a generic rubric and a closed-book LLM-written one — the retrieval, not the rubric format, carries the gain.

Adding evolution on top is worth roughly two more points, with the gap widening the longer training runs — consistent with the claim that evolving rubrics capture knowledge the policy discovers along the way. Both beat a random-reward control, which rules out the spurious-reward effect that has embarrassed other Qwen-based RL results.

One result matters for anyone thinking about adopting this: swapping GPT-4.1 for Qwen3-8B — the policy's own base model — as both rubric generator and judge still yields +4.4 points over SFT, against +5.7 with the GPT judge. The gains are not distilled from a stronger proprietary grader. The generation–verification gap comes from the privileged information, not from the judge being smarter than the student.

The other implementation

Kimi K3 puts rubric-writing inside the judge's protocol

K3 reserves its Agentic Generative Reward Model for the non-verifiable remainder of post-training — most of its RL runs in verifiable agentic environments with real checkers. The GRM keeps K2.5's tournament-style group reward over binary comparisons, but the judge is now an agent bound to a four-step procedure it must follow for every comparison:

  1. Read the outcome, product, or text output. The judge is agentic because the artifact under review may be a spreadsheet, a web app, or a multi-day deliverable — not a paragraph.
  2. Generate a rubric. Written fresh, conditioned on what the candidates actually produced, rather than retrieved from a fixed bank.
  3. Score each candidate against it.
  4. Record the rubric-assigned scores in a scorepad. An auditable trace of why one candidate beat the other.

K3 hardens against the same failure mode from the other direction. Instead of a negative rubric penalizing verbosity, there is a hard budget: given a baseline output length ℓ₀ from the cold-start model and a multiplier σ, any candidate exceeding σ·ℓ₀ automatically loses the comparison. It is the reward-hacking patch expressed as a rule rather than a learned criterion.

Side by side

Same diagnosis, different point in the pipeline

Both systems refuse to fix criteria in advance and both make the reward model author them at judgment time. Where they part ways is whether the rubric is a persistent training artifact or a transient inference step.

So is the claim right?

Yes, with one correction of emphasis. Both papers respond to the same insight — predefined rubrics cannot keep up with an improving model on tasks nobody can verify — and both hand rubric authorship to the grader.

But "generates its own rubrics on the fly" describes K3 precisely and DR Tulu only partially. DR Tulu's contribution is not that rubrics are generated; it is that they are kept, scored for discriminative power, and pruned. Evolution is a property of the training loop, not of any single judgment. K3's judge is dynamic; DR Tulu's rubric buffer is adaptive. The distinction is exactly the two points that ablation is worth.

A footnote worth noticing: K3 reports state of the art on ResearchRubrics (76.2), a benchmark built entirely out of prompts and rubrics for grading deep research agents. The evaluation regime and the training regime have converged on the same instrument.
