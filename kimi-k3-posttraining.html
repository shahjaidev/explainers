Train nine models. Ship one.

K3's post-training doesn't optimize a single policy. It trains three domain specialists at three reasoning-effort levels — nine expert models — and then distils all nine back into one student on-policy. Everything else here is about the environments and data that make those nine experts worth having.

Structure

The three stages

The shape is conventional; the scale and the consolidation step are not.

Stage 1 · §4.1.1
Supervised fine-tuning

Establishes a cold-start policy with baseline agentic competence. Trajectories synthesized by domain-specialized models from earlier Kimi generations, then multi-stage verified and human-annotated.

Stage 2 · §4.1.2
Reinforcement learning

Nine experts: three broad domains × three reasoning-effort levels. This is where the capability actually comes from.

Stage 3 · §4.1.3
Multi-teacher on-policy distillation

Consolidates nine specialists into one unified model, teacher selected per sampled (domain, effort) pair.

Running underneath all three: quantisation-aware training from stage 1 onward, so the model that ships is the model that trained.

§4.1.1

Supervised fine-tuning

SFT's job here is narrow and explicitly stated: produce a cold start good enough that RL has something worth optimizing. The report is direct that SFT gives "a solid cold-start foundation" but that RL is what unlocks higher-order reasoning and execution.

The interesting part is where the data comes from. Rather than human demonstrations at scale, trajectories are synthesized by domain-specialized models from the prior Kimi series — K3's cold start is distilled from a fleet of narrower predecessors — then put through multi-stage verification and human-in-the-loop annotation. The expansion over K2/K2.5's pipeline is targeted specifically at complex agentic tasks.

Two details carry more weight than their length suggests. XTML (eXtensible Token Markup Language) is the chat template every trajectory is serialized with — long-horizon agentic traces mix reasoning, tool calls, tool results and multimodal observations, and they need one consistent representation across SFT, RL rollouts and production. And QAT starts here, not at the end: MXFP4 weights with MXFP8 activations run through the entire post-training stage, so the model spends all of RL adapting to its deployment precision.

§4.1.2

Reinforcement learning across nine cells

The design decision worth noticing is what K3 didn't do: train a specialized RL model per task. Instead RL is scaled across three broad domains, each covering a wide spectrum of sub-tasks, and one expert is trained per domain at every reasoning-effort level.

3 domains × 3 efforts = 9 teachers

click a cell

Scaling RL FLOPs does two things at once, and the report presents them as inseparable: tool-call step counts scale up consistently, and capability improves across the board — coding experience, general tool use, web development, agentic search, professional workflows, office deliverables, chart understanding, visual puzzles. The model isn't just getting more accurate; it's learning to take more steps before answering.

Algorithm

Partial rollout: refusing to wait for stragglers

Long-horizon RL has a brutal latency distribution. Sample K completions for each of N prompts and the iteration is held hostage by whichever trajectory decides to take a thousand tool calls. K3 extends the partial-rollout scheme from its synchronous framework: generation pauses as soon as a fraction λ of trajectories have finished, and the unfinished ones are enqueued and prioritized for resumption next iteration.

This creates a real problem: an individual long-horizon trajectory now spans multiple iterations, so by the time it's optimized it was generated by a policy several updates old. That's an extreme off-policy regime, and it's exactly what destabilizes naive RL. K3's answer is a per-token regularization that constrains updates to a localized neighbourhood, which the report credits with tolerating highly stale data while keeping training stable.

Partial rollout only works because the sandbox can be frozen mid-task and thawed an iteration later. The algorithm and the microVM infrastructure are one design, not two.

Reasoning-effort RL

Effort levels aren't a decoding-time knob bolted on afterwards — they're trained. Each problem x gets an initial token budget b₀(x) estimated from the cold-start model. Any trajectory whose total budget exceeds τ·b₀(x) has its task reward overridden with −1, regardless of whether it got the answer right.

What counts toward the budget differs by task type, which is a nice detail: for general tasks it's thinking tokens; for agentic tasks it's cumulative output tokens including tool-call arguments. An agent can't game the budget by moving its reasoning into the arguments it writes.

The budget cliff

reward as a function of trajectory length

The cliff is what makes this work as a preference rather than a penalty: below the threshold the model is free to think as much as it likes and is scored purely on the task; one token over and the trajectory is worthless. Annealing τ downward is what converts a max-effort expert into a high- and then low-effort one.

Non-verifiable rewards

The agentic generative reward model

For tasks with no ground truth, K3 keeps K2.5's tournament-style group reward with binary comparisons — but the judge is now an agent, and it's held to a mandatory four-step protocol rather than asked for an opinion.

Forcing the rubric to be written before the candidates are scored, and recorded in a scorepad, is a straightforward anti-rationalization measure. The verbosity control is the same budget idea from reasoning-effort RL pointed at a different failure mode: reward hacking toward ever-longer outputs. A candidate exceeding σ·ℓ₀ automatically loses its comparison.

§4.1.3

Consolidating nine experts into one

Nine specialists is a research artifact, not a product. Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation collapses them: for each training sample, a domain d and an effort level e are sampled, and the corresponding expert π(d,e) supervises the student — on the student's own rollouts.

The reward is a clipped per-token log-ratio between teacher and student:

rdopd(yt | e, x, y<t) = clip( sg( log [ π(d,e)teacher(yt) / πθ(yt | e) ] ), −Rmax, +Rmax )

The clip matters for the same reason it does anywhere in RL — an unbounded log-ratio on a rare token produces an advantage spike that wrecks the update. But the structural point is the one the report emphasizes: because the distillation signal is expressed as a dense per-token reward, it drops into the existing RL machinery unchanged. Partial rollout, the sandbox infrastructure, the off-policy tolerance — MOPD inherits all of it instead of needing a separate training path.

Related · is this OPSD?

MOPD is often filed under "on-policy self-distillation." It isn't: every teacher here is a separate RL expert, never the student conditioned on privileged context. That distinction — and why the research literature argues the privileged-self version is unstable — is the subject of a companion page.

§4.1.4

Post-training that knows how it will be served

MXFP4 quantisation-aware training

MoE expert weights dominate parameter memory, so those go to MXFP4 with MXFP8 activations, while everything else — attention projections, latent MoE projections, shared experts, routers — stays at higher precision. QAT runs through all of post-training, SFT and RL alike.

The consequence is worth stating plainly: during RL, rollout and training share the same quantisation scheme. The usual train–inference mismatch, where a model is optimized in one numeric regime and deployed in another, is eliminated by construction rather than patched afterwards.

Turning the MTP layer into a draft model

K3 is pre-trained with a multi-token-prediction layer that mirrors a backbone block. An EAGLE-3 draft model is a single decoder layer with the same structure — so K3 fine-tunes the MTP layer into the draft, freezing the target and updating only the draft layer and its feature-fusion projection.

The W_E3 = [0 0 I] initialization is a small piece of craft: it makes the fused representation start out identical to the high-level feature the MTP layer already knows how to consume, so fine-tuning begins from a working draft rather than a scrambled one, and the low- and mid-level features get incorporated gradually.

The loss choice is the substantive one. Speculative decoding's speedup is governed by the acceptance rate Σ min(p,q). Minimizing KL — the conventional surrogate — does not maximize that quantity for a capacity-limited draft, so K3 optimizes the negative log of the acceptance rate directly.

§4.2 — the data half

Environments are the training data

For an agentic model, "data" mostly isn't a corpus. It's environments that can be instantiated, acted in, and scored. K3's §4.2 is a catalogue of purpose-built ones, and it's where most of the interesting engineering lives.

The unified white-box harness

Training inside one fixed agent harness teaches a model that harness — its tool schema, its system prompt, its context-management strategy. K3 instead represents a harness as a collection of configurable, composable modules: tool interfaces, system prompts, context management, skills, memories, subagents. Compose them one way and you get Kimi Code; compose them another and you get Claude Code, Codex, OpenClaw, Hermes — or something that has never existed.

Compose a harness

toggle modules · RL varies these per task group

During RL, harness configurations are constructed dynamically per task group, so the model sees many combinations rather than the conventions of any one scaffold. The same abstraction is what lets a single RL framework span every task domain below.

§4.2.2

Knowledge-graph-guided task synthesis

Task quality is bounded by source-material quality, and source material is found by search — so the leverage is in the queries. Fine-grained concepts surface specialized, underrepresented knowledge; sampling across diverse concepts broadens coverage. K3 controls both by building a self-evolving, hierarchically organized knowledge graph that agents expand through web-scale exploration.

Granularity controls what you find

click a node to sample a query from it

Two design choices do the real work. Edges are always directed coarse → fine regardless of which endpoint the agent found first, which keeps the hierarchy meaningful rather than an artifact of crawl order. And an agent explores the existing graph before adding anything, reusing equivalent nodes — without that, web-scale recursive expansion degenerates into thousands of near-duplicate concepts.

§4.2.3 – §4.2.7

Five families of training environment

§4.2.4Kernel optimisation — where reward hacking is the hard part＋

A large suite spanning single-operator kernels to fused mega-kernels, sourced from high-quality GitHub repositories such as Flash Linear Attention. It covers CUDA, Triton, CuTe DSL, Gluon, ThunderKittens and TileLang, across widely used GPU architectures and BF16 / FP8 / FP4.

The reward is graded rather than binary — and the anti-cheating system is treated as a first-class, continuously extended component, because a kernel task is unusually easy to fake.

§4.2.3Verifiable problems in agentic environments＋

Three representative shapes. Multi-step complex information search — the model plans its research, gathers web evidence step by step, and produces a verifiable answer. Real professional work — investment banking, data analysis, legal practice: decompose a complex request, operate domain tools in a sandbox, produce a deliverable over dozens to hundreds of steps. Multi-step verifiable visual reasoning over STEM problems, visual puzzles and charts.

The visual track is the most architecturally interesting, because it's the pre-training multimodal bet cashing out. Each trajectory runs in an agent environment with a Python interpreter in an isolated sandbox: the model writes and executes code to crop, zoom or transform the input image, compute precisely, or verify intermediate results — and receives execution outputs, including generated images, as new observations. As it learns to perform more image operations and gather more observations, complex-visual-reasoning performance steadily improves. That is the mechanism behind ZeroBench going 23.0 → 41.0 with a Python tool.

§4.2.5Personal assistant tasks — living environments＋

Realistic mock implementations of Gmail, Notion, Slack and Canvas, preserving the core semantics of the real applications while allowing reproducible, large-scale interaction with no external APIs or rate limits.

On top of those, tasks modelled on real professional workflows in HR, legal services and finance. The agent operates in a persistent, evolving environment over multiple simulated days, encountering dozens of interdependent events distributed across applications. Each event carries its own evaluation criterion, assessed by deterministic rules or LLM evaluators.

Scale of a single rollout: up to thousands of tool calls and millions of context tokens. The initial workspace itself is built by agents that search the web for reference material and transform it into a coherent, task-relevant environment — and the RL framework had to be extended to model event streams and the world-state transitions they induce.

§4.2.6Autonomous Execution Tasks — verify in the loop＋

AET gives the agent an initial state, a constrained goal, a tool-based action space, execution budgets, and an independent verifier. What it deliberately withholds is everything else: no reference trajectories, no predefined procedures. The agent must do its own decomposition, tool selection, planning, error recovery and termination.

Crucially, reward is grounded in the verifier's assessment of the final environment state — not the agent's self-reported completion.

Verifier types span black-box system replication, quantitative factor discovery and tax auditing. In the report's flagship example — reconstructing a hidden 3D-camera repair system as a web application through oracle queries alone — K3 reaches 1.000 completion where Opus 4.8 reaches 0.918, GPT-5.5 0.893, and Kimi K2.6 only 0.560.

§4.2.7Web development — deterministic checks plus a judge that looks＋

Expert-curated tasks whose inputs range from one-line scene descriptions to multi-paragraph specifications, producing websites, interactive games, 3D/WebGL scenes, data visualisations, SVGs and full-stack applications. Every task runs in a containerised sandbox and is rolled out under diverse agent scaffolds rather than a single fixed harness, to force cross-scaffold generalisation.

The three zeroing conditions carry the design: a project that fails to build, errors at runtime, or fakes rather than implements the artifact scores nothing. The last one is the anti-hacking clause — an agent that stubs out the hard part and returns something that looks right gets no credit.
