PipelineRL: never stop generating

RL for long reasoning chains has an ugly trade-off: either you keep your GPUs busy by learning from stale, off-policy data, or you stay on-policy and let half the cluster sit idle. PipelineRL's answer is to swap the model's weights mid-sentence, inside the inference engine, every optimizer step. Same reward, about twice as fast.

Six things to remember

If you only read one tab, read this one. Each card is one idea from the paper, in the order the argument builds.

01 · the problem

Long generations starve the GPUs

In conventional RL you generate a batch, then train on it, then repeat. Short answers finish first, so the batch of in-flight sequences shrinks toward a few stragglers and accelerator utilization collapses. Throughput only grows roughly linearly up to a generation batch of about 128 sequences per server, so you cannot just throw more GPUs at a small batch.

02 · the usual fix, and its cost

Big batches buy throughput with staleness

The standard workaround is to generate a huge batch and take G optimizer steps on it. Larger G means more throughput, but by step G the policy has drifted G updates away from the one that produced the data. The paper calls this gap the lag, and off-policy data learns worse per sample.

03 · the idea

In-flight weight updates

Run generation and training concurrently on separate GPUs. After every optimizer step, broadcast the new weights to the inference servers over NCCL. The servers pause only for the time it takes to receive them, then keep decoding the same sequences with new weights. No preemption, no discarded partial rollouts, no KV cache recomputation.

04 · the surprising part

Stale KV caches are fine

After a swap, the keys and values already in the cache were computed by the old weights. One might expect this to break stability. It does not. The paper measures the KL between these "mixed-policy" sequences and truly on-policy ones and finds it tracks on-policy closely, while conventional lagged data diverges more and more as lag grows.

05 · staying honest

Per-token log-probs plus a max-lag valve

Each generated token stores the log-probability under whatever weights produced it. The trainer uses these for an importance-sampling ratio, clipped on one side. A single knob, max_lag, caps how far ahead the trainer may run of the slowest in-flight rollout, so the system cannot silently drift off-policy.

06 · the payoff

Same sample efficiency, double the speed

PipelineRL matches the per-sample learning of conventional RL with G = 8 or 16, while producing samples about twice as fast as the G = 32 baseline. Net: the same reward in roughly half the wall-clock time, and 7B and 32B models that match or exceed Open-Reasoner-Zero on AIME-2024 and MATH-500.

The whole paper is one bet: the cost of a weight swap inside a running sequence is far smaller than the cost of either stopping the sequence or training on stale data.

Why this mattered beyond the paper

Meta's ScaleRL study, which burned over 400,000 GPU-hours comparing RL recipes, adopted PipelineRL as its asynchrony scheme and reported it reached about the same asymptotic reward as off-policy PPO with much better compute efficiency. They ablated the off-policyness setting, found 4 and 8 steps of lag performed equally well, and defaulted to 8. Mistral's Magistral describes essentially the same trick. By late 2025 "in-flight weight updates" was a standard phrase in the RL systems vocabulary, and vLLM shipped first-class support for pausing and resuming generation around a weight load.

Two kinds of waste. Inside each generation phase the batch thins out as short sequences finish, so accelerators run far below their efficient batch size. Between phases, whichever pool is not active waits. Making G bigger amortizes the waits, but the last of the G steps trains on data that is G updates stale.

PipelineRL
generation never drains; weights are pushed in-flight after every optimizer step
generatingtrainingweight-update pause

As soon as a sequence finishes, a new prompt takes its slot, so the inference servers sit at their optimal batch size continuously. Finished sequences stream to the trainer. Each optimizer step ends with a broadcast; the servers pause, load the tensors, and resume the very same in-progress sequences under the new weights.

What one sequence looks like

Because weights change while a sequence is still being decoded, a single rollout can contain tokens produced by several model versions. The paper calls these mixed-policy sequences: the earliest tokens are the most off-policy, the latest are nearly on-policy. Each token carries the log-probability under the weights that actually sampled it, which is exactly what the trainer needs to correct for the mismatch.

A mixed-policy rollout
one 8k-token reasoning chain spanning three weight versions

The KV cache is not recomputed after a swap. The paper tested a variant that does recompute it ("PipelineRL with KV cache recomputed") and found the plain version's divergence from on-policy data stays close anyway, at three different stages of training (checkpoints 0, 100 and 190 of a 222-step run).

The lag valve, as implemented

Asynchrony needs a brake. In the code, the actor process is allowed to have at most one update's worth of prompts plus max_lag extra samples in flight ahead of the trainer's latest broadcast version. When it hits that limit it stops submitting new prompts until the next weight version arrives. The trainer, on its side, counts how many samples arrived with a version more than max_lag behind the one it last broadcast. With max_lag = 0 the system degenerates to synchronous RL; the larger it is, the more slack the generators have. (Read from the code, not the paper text.)

Lag intuition
illustrative, not paper data: how G and in-flight updates shape per-token staleness

Left bar: in conventional RL every token in the batch is used for G optimizer steps, so staleness climbs to G − 1 by the last step. Right bar: in PipelineRL a token's staleness is bounded by how many updates landed after it was sampled, and the valve caps that. Conventional staleness is uniform across a sequence; PipelineRL staleness is concentrated in the earliest tokens.

The math, as simply as possible

There are only four equations you need. Each one gets the formula, then a plain-English translation, then why PipelineRL cares about it.

1 · What "lag" means

lag(token)  =  vtrainer  −  vsampled

Where: vsampled is the weight version that produced the token; vtrainer is the version the trainer holds when it finally uses that token in a gradient.

In words Lag is how many optimizer steps the model has taken since this piece of data was generated. Zero lag is pure on-policy. The paper's framing: when you take G optimizer steps per batch of data, the current policy π gets ahead of the behavior policy μ that generated the data, and lag is the number of steps between them. In conventional RL lag is uniform within a batch and reaches G − 1. In PipelineRL it varies token by token, and max_lag caps it.

2 · The two quantities every RL system trades off

ΔRΔt  =  ΔRΔS  ×  ΔSΔt

Reward gained per second equals reward gained per sample (learning effectiveness) times samples generated per second (throughput efficiency).

In words This is the whole paper in one line. Conventional on-policy RL (G = 1) has the best reward per sample because nothing is stale, but terrible samples per second because the batch keeps draining. Cranking G up fixes the second factor and damages the first. Adding more accelerators raises throughput linearly but, as the paper notes, may eventually hurt learning effectiveness because sequences become more mixed-policy. PipelineRL's claim is that it keeps the first factor at the G = 8 level while roughly doubling the second relative to G = 32.

3 · The advantage: how good was this answer, relative to its siblings?

Ai  =  ri  −  1n − 1 ∑j ≠ i rj

Where: a group of n attempts (default 8) is sampled for the same prompt, each gets a reward r (1 for a verified-correct answer, 0 otherwise in the pure-success setting), and attempt i's advantage is its reward minus the mean reward of the other attempts. Optionally divided by the group's standard deviation, off by default.

In words This is GRPO's group baseline, with the leave-one-out twist: an attempt is not compared against an average that includes itself. If 2 of 8 attempts were right, a correct one gets +1 − 1/7 ≈ +0.86, a wrong one gets 0 − 2/7 ≈ −0.29. No value network is needed. The README is explicit that the algorithm is a "simplified GRPO: no value network, no trust-region clamping, no KL or entropy bonuses by default."

4 · The loss: REINFORCE with a one-sided importance-sampling brake

L  =  − ∑tokens t  log πθ(yt)  ·  A  ·  min( πθ(yt)μ(yt),  1 + ε)stop-grad

Where: πθ is the current trainer policy, μ is whichever weights actually sampled token t (its stored log-prob), A is the advantage from equation 3, and ε is a small clip (0.02 in the released config). The ratio is treated as a constant, so gradients flow only through log πθ. Read from the reinforce branch of the code's RL step; a standard PPO clipped-surrogate branch is also available.

In words Start from the simplest policy gradient: push up the log-probability of tokens in good answers, push it down in bad ones, weighted by the advantage. Then multiply each token's contribution by an importance weight, the ratio of how likely the current model is to say that token versus how likely the sampling model was. If the token came from a slightly older model, the ratio corrects for that. The ratio is clipped from above at 1 + ε, so a token the new model now loves much more than the old one did cannot dominate the gradient. It is not clipped from below: tokens the new model has moved away from are simply down-weighted toward zero. This is why staleness is survivable: the correction is applied per token, using the exact weights that produced each token, which is precisely the information mixed-policy sequences preserve.

How do you know it stayed on-policy? Effective sample size

wi = πθ(yi)μ(yi)     ESS  =  1N  ·  (∑ wi)2∑ wi2

Where: the importance weights w are the same ratios as in the loss, and ESS is normalised so that 1.0 means "as good as N fresh on-policy samples."

In words If all weights are 1 (perfectly on-policy), ESS is 1. If a few tokens have huge weights and the rest tiny ones, the sum of squares blows up and ESS collapses toward 0: you effectively have only a handful of useful samples. The paper reports PipelineRL's ESS stays around the level of conventional RL with G = 8 (at least 0.93), even though its maximum lag is far higher, with some tokens over 50k samples stale. High max lag with high ESS is the signature of the method: a few early tokens are very stale, but the bulk of the data is fresh.

Optional terms, off by default

The code supports a KL penalty to a frozen reference model, computed with the unbiased estimator exp(r) − r − 1 where r = log π_ref − log π_θ, with the log-ratio clamped to ±5 for numerical safety, and an entropy bonus. Both coefficients are zero in the released configs. A difficulty-aware length penalty and an overlong-response filter also exist as options. None of these are what makes PipelineRL work; they are standard knobs the authors left available.

The whole algorithm in eight lines

# trainer loop (one process group); actors and vLLM servers run concurrently elsewhere
while training:
    batch = next_ready_samples()                 # streamed from actors; each token carries μ-logprob and version
    count_if(version_now - batch.version > max_lag)   # monitored, not dropped
    A     = reward - leave_one_out_group_mean(reward)
    ratio = exp(logp_θ - logp_μ).clamp(max=1 + ε).detach()
    loss  = -(logp_θ * A * ratio).sum()  [- kl_coef * KL(π_ref ‖ π_θ)]
    optimizer.step();  version_now += 1
    nccl_broadcast(weights)  # servers pause ≈ ms, load, resume the same in-flight sequences

Mixed-policy sequences are close to on-policy

To check that swapping weights mid-sequence does not quietly produce weird text, the authors measured KL divergence between sequences generated under PipelineRL's mixed behavior policy and sequences from the fully on-policy model, at three training stages (checkpoints 0, 100 and 190 of a 222-step run). They compared against conventional off-policy data at various lags k, and against a PipelineRL variant that recomputes the KV cache after each swap. Mixed-policy sequences stayed close to on-policy at all three stages. Conventional lagged sequences diverged more and more as k grew. Recomputing the KV cache was not needed.

Caveats the paper is honest about

  • Throughput scales linearly with more generation GPUs, but learning effectiveness may eventually suffer because more updates land inside each sequence, making early tokens more stale.
  • The lag valve is a hyperparameter. ScaleRL later found 4 and 8 steps of lag indistinguishable and chose 8; the right value depends on the trainer-to-generator speed ratio.
  • Later work (for example variance-controlled off-policy methods in 2026) argues that at large lag the one-sided clipped ratio still lets ESS collapse, and proposes stronger corrections. PipelineRL's own numbers stay in the regime where ESS is high.
